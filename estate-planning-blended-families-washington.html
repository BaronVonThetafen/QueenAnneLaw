Estate Planning for Blended Families: Why a Simple Plan May Not Be Enough

Blended families are common, but estate planning for them can be surprisingly complicated.

You might have children from a prior marriage. Your spouse might have children of their own. Perhaps you have children together as well. You may consider all of them your children, regardless of biology.

The law, however, does not necessarily view those relationships in the same way.

Under Washington's intestacy statute, property passing without a will is distributed according to a statutory order that includes a surviving spouse or registered domestic partner and the decedent's own issue and other relatives. Stepchildren are not automatically treated as the decedent's children under the ordinary intestacy rules.

That makes intentional estate planning particularly important for blended families.

Your Stepchildren May Receive Nothing From Your Estate

One of the most important issues for a blended family is also one of the easiest to overlook.

You may have helped raise your stepchild for twenty years. You may think of that person as your son or daughter. You may have celebrated holidays together, helped pay for college, and watched their children grow up.

But your relationship alone does not necessarily give that stepchild the inheritance rights that your biological or legally adopted children would ordinarily have.

Washington law contains a narrow provision involving inheritance from a stepparent in unusual circumstances where property would otherwise escheat. Families should not rely on that limited rule as a substitute for an estate plan.

If you want a stepchild to inherit from you, your estate plan should say so.

The “Everything to My Spouse” Problem

There is another potential problem, even when both spouses have wills.

Consider a common scenario. You have two children from a previous marriage. You remarry and create a simple will leaving everything to your new spouse. Your will then provides that if your spouse has already died, the estate goes to your children.

You die first, and your spouse inherits the estate.

At that point, the assets belong to your spouse. Your spouse may later change their estate plan. They may leave the remaining assets to their own children, a new spouse, another relative, or someone else entirely. Even if your spouse intends to provide for your children, circumstances can change over the years.

The result may be very different from what you intended when you signed your will.

A Trust Can Provide for a Spouse and Protect an Inheritance for Children

For some blended families, a trust offers a way to balance two important goals:

  • Supporting a surviving spouse
  • Preserving assets for children or other beneficiaries after the surviving spouse dies

Instead of leaving an entire estate outright to a surviving spouse, a will or revocable living trust can provide that certain assets are held in trust after the first spouse's death.

Depending on how the trust is drafted, the surviving spouse might receive income or distributions for health, support, or other specified needs. When the surviving spouse dies, the remaining trust property can pass to the beneficiaries selected by the first spouse.

That structure can provide the surviving spouse with financial security without relying entirely on the surviving spouse's later estate plan to protect the first spouse's children.

Example: A Queen Anne Blended Family

Imagine a married couple living in Queen Anne.

Erin has two adult children from her first marriage. Her husband, David, has one adult daughter from his first marriage. They own their home together and have retirement accounts, investment accounts, and other property.

Erin wants David to be financially secure if she dies first. But she also wants to make certain that some of her estate ultimately passes to her two children.

If Erin simply leaves everything outright to David, she gives up substantial control over where those assets eventually go. Instead, her estate plan could provide that some assets pass outright to David while other assets are held in trust for his benefit during his lifetime, with the remaining trust property ultimately distributed to Erin's children.

That allows Erin to address both goals rather than choosing between her spouse and her children.

Blended Families Should Also Review Beneficiary Designations

Your will or trust is only part of the estate-planning picture.

Many significant assets can pass according to a beneficiary designation or other nonprobate arrangement rather than under the terms of a will. That can include:

  • Retirement accounts
  • Life insurance policies
  • Certain investment or financial accounts
  • Payable-on-death or transfer-on-death accounts
  • Property owned with survivorship rights

A beautifully drafted will does not solve the problem if the beneficiary designations direct the assets somewhere else. For blended families, coordinating those designations with the overall estate plan is especially important.

Be Specific About Who You Mean by “My Children”

Estate-planning documents for blended families should be deliberate about terminology.

If you want biological children, adopted children, and particular stepchildren to share equally, the documents should clearly express that intention. Likewise, if different children are supposed to receive different property, the plan should say so.

You should not have to depend on family members to interpret what you “probably meant” after you are gone.

Fair Does Not Always Mean Equal

Blended-family estate planning also raises an important personal question: What does fair mean for your family?

It does not necessarily mean dividing every asset equally among every child and stepchild. One spouse may have brought substantially more property into the marriage. One child may have already received significant financial assistance. A stepchild may have inherited from another parent. A surviving spouse may need the home and income for the remainder of their life.

There is no single correct solution. The goal is to make those choices intentionally rather than allowing default inheritance rules or outdated beneficiary designations to make them for you.

Talk About the Plan Before It Becomes a Problem

Estate planning cannot eliminate every family disagreement. But ambiguity can make disagreements much worse.

A good blended-family estate plan should answer questions such as:

  • Who should inherit from you?
  • Do you want your stepchildren included?
  • How much should a surviving spouse receive outright?
  • Should some property remain in trust?
  • Who should manage that trust?
  • When should children or stepchildren receive their inheritance?
  • Who should inherit if an intended beneficiary dies before you?
  • Do your retirement accounts and life insurance beneficiary designations match the plan?

Those decisions are often easier to make while everyone is healthy and relationships are stable.

Planning for the Family You Actually Have

A blended family does not fit neatly into a one-size-fits-all estate plan.

You may want to protect your spouse. You may want to ensure that children from a prior relationship are not unintentionally disinherited. You may consider a stepchild to be your own child and want your estate plan to reflect that relationship.

Wills and trusts allow you to create a plan around the family you actually have, rather than leaving those decisions to Washington's default inheritance rules.
